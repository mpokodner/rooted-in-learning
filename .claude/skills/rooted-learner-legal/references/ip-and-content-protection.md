## 1. Copyright basics and why registration matters most

Copyright attaches automatically the moment an original work is fixed in tangible form. No registration required for the right to exist. But two provisions of the Copyright Act make registration the single highest-return legal action available to this business:

- **17 U.S.C. §411(a)** — a U.S. work must be registered before an infringement suit can be filed. No registration, no lawsuit.
- **17 U.S.C. §412** — **statutory damages and attorney's fees** are available only if the work was registered **before the infringement began**, or within **three months of first publication**. Without timely registration, the only recovery is actual damages plus the infringer's profits, which for a $12 print pack is functionally zero — and no attorney will take that case on contingency.

**What this means concretely.** If someone strips the branding off "Voices That Carried a Code" and resells it, timely registration is the difference between a credible demand letter backed by statutory damages (up to $30,000 per work, up to $150,000 for willful infringement) and a letter with nothing behind it. The registration fee is modest. The protection is not.

**Practical registration strategy for a curriculum publisher:**

- Register **groups of unpublished works** in a single application where eligible — the Copyright Office permits group registration for certain categories, which brings the per-work cost down substantially. Check current group registration options at copyright.gov before filing; the categories and limits change.
- Register **within three months of publication**, every time. Build it into the product launch checklist alongside the TPT upload. A quarterly batch is realistic for someone with 8–10 hours a week.
- Register the **compilation** as well as individual works where a print pack has independently valuable selection and arrangement.
- Keep **dated drafts and version history** — provenance evidence matters if authorship is ever contested, and it matters even more given §2 and §3 below.
- Put a **copyright notice** on every page: `© 2026 The Rooted Learner LLC. All rights reserved.` Notice isn't required for protection but it defeats an innocent-infringement defense that can reduce damages.

Register in the name of **The Rooted Learner LLC**, not Michelle personally, and make sure there's a written assignment from Michelle to the LLC for anything she authored personally before or outside the LLC. Ownership gaps between founder and entity are a standard diligence finding.

## 2. Work-for-hire and the HCPSS employment problem

**This is the most underrated legal risk in the business, and it needs to be raised whenever curriculum authorship comes up.**

Under 17 U.S.C. §101 and §201(b), a work prepared by an employee **within the scope of employment** is a work made for hire, and **the employer is the author and owner** — automatically, with no assignment required. Michelle is a full-time K-8 teacher at an HCPSS school. She produces Grade 5 ELA instructional materials. Those two facts sit uncomfortably close together.

The business skill currently states: *"All work created on own time with own resources belongs to Michelle / The Rooted Learner LLC."* That is the right principle and the correct goal — but it is **not automatically true as a matter of law**. Scope of employment under the *Restatement* factors and *CCNV v. Reid* (490 U.S. 730 (1989)) turns on whether the work is of the kind she is employed to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits, and is actuated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. Creating fifth-grade ELA materials is unambiguously "the kind of work she is employed to perform." That's the exposure.

There is a **"teacher exception"** in pre-1976 common law — the tradition that academic authors own their scholarly output. Its survival under the 1976 Act is genuinely unsettled; it has been discussed favorably in dicta (notably by Judge Posner in *Hays v. Sony Corp. of America*, 847 F.2d 412 (7th Cir. 1988), and in *Weinstein v. University of Illinois*, 811 F.2d 1091 (7th Cir. 1987)) but never squarely resolved, and its application to K-12 teachers rather than university faculty is weaker still. **Do not rely on it.**

### What to actually do

**Read the paper first.** Before anything else, Michelle needs to know what her own documents say:
- The HCPSS employee handbook and board policies on intellectual property and outside employment
- The negotiated agreement between HCPSS and the teachers' association (HCEA) — IP and outside-work provisions are often in there
- Anything she signed at hire or during onboarding
- Any grant, curriculum-writing stipend, or committee agreement — **paid curriculum-writing work for the district is almost certainly work for hire, full stop**

**Then build the separation record.** These are evidentiary facts that support her ownership claim, and they only help if they're true and documented:
- Create only on personal time — nights, weekends, breaks, and never during contract hours
- Personal devices, personal accounts, personal cloud storage, personal email. Never a district laptop, district Google account, district network, or district printer
- Never use district-purchased curriculum, district assessment data, district-licensed images or fonts, or district-developed materials as source content
- Never use her own students' work, responses, or data — see also the risk register
- Never pilot or use the product in her own classroom without written district authorization
- Keep timestamped version history showing off-hours creation
- Keep business expenses genuinely separate — the LLC pays for the software, the fonts, the hosting

**Consider asking for written acknowledgment.** Some districts will issue a letter confirming that outside-created materials are the employee's own. Whether to ask is a strategic judgment — it resolves the ambiguity, but it also puts the question in front of HCPSS, who may not have thought about it. That's a decision to make with counsel, weighing the current stage of the business.

**The conflict-of-interest layer is separate and equally real.** HCPSS's outside employment policy already bars consulting with HCPSS. Beyond that, most district ethics policies restrict using position or district resources for private gain, and restrict employees from marketing to colleagues. Selling to the teacher next door is a different problem than selling to a district in another county — and it's the kind of thing that gets noticed.

### Take to counsel
- Whether HCPSS's employment documents, board policy, or the HCEA negotiated agreement contain an IP assignment reaching materials created on personal time.
- Whether any past district curriculum-writing work created co-ownership in materials now being commercialized.
- Whether to seek written acknowledgment from HCPSS, and how to frame the request.
- Whether the conflict-of-interest policy restricts marketing to Maryland districts generally or only HCPSS.

## 3. AI-assisted content — what is actually protectable

The products are AI-assisted. The passages, questions, and overlays are generated with Claude. This directly affects what can be registered and owned, and it is not a hypothetical concern — it's the central IP question for this business.

**The current state of the law:**

- **Human authorship is required.** *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 130 F.4th 1039 (D.C. Cir. 2025), affirmed that the Copyright Act requires a human author; rehearing en banc was denied in May 2025. Purely AI-generated material is not copyrightable.
- **Prompts alone are not enough.** The Copyright Office's January 2025 Copyrightability report concluded that, with generally available technology, prompts alone do not give the user sufficient control to make them the author of the output — no matter how detailed or iterative the prompting. The *Théâtre D'opéra Spatial* denial (September 2023) is the direct precedent: elaborate prompting and subsequent modification, still denied, partly for failure to disclaim the AI-generated portions.
- **Human contributions around the AI output are protectable.** *Zarya of the Dawn* (February 2023) is the model: the AI-generated images were excluded, but the human-authored text and the human selection and arrangement of images and text were registrable.
- **Registration requires disclosure.** Applicants must describe the human authorship and **disclaim AI-generated material** that is more than de minimis. Failing to disclose can invalidate a registration. Where a registration has already issued without disclosure, the fix is supplementary registration.

**What this means for The Rooted Learner:**

The protectable layer is almost certainly *not* the raw generated passage text. It is:
- The **selection and arrangement** — which standards, which passages, which items, in what sequence
- **Substantial human editing and rewriting** of generated text, documented
- The **pedagogical apparatus** — rubrics, teacher notes, answer rationales, ELD scaffolds, the standards-alignment logic
- The **visual design and layout** — the print pack design system is human-authored and independently protectable
- The **compilation** as a whole

**Operational recommendations:**

1. **Keep an authorship record per product.** What was generated, what Michelle wrote or substantially rewrote, what editorial judgments she made. This is boring to maintain and it is exactly what a registration application and any future dispute will need.
2. **Edit substantially and document it.** Version history showing meaningful human revision is the evidence. Accepting generated text unchanged is the weakest position.
3. **Disclaim honestly on registration applications.** Describe the human contribution in "Author Created," disclaim AI-generated material in "Material Excluded."
4. **Don't overclaim in marketing.** "© The Rooted Learner, all rights reserved" on the compilation is fine. Claiming exclusive rights in text that is largely unedited machine output is not, and it's the kind of representation that becomes a problem if the business is ever acquired or audited.
5. **Check the AI provider's terms.** Providers generally assign output rights to the customer, but customer ownership of outputs from the provider is a separate question from whether copyright subsists in them at all — the provider cannot grant what the law doesn't recognize. Verify current commercial terms.
6. **The competitive reality:** if the passages themselves aren't strongly protectable, the moat is the brand, the design system, the standards-alignment methodology, the district relationships, and the software — not the text. That's an argument for investing in trademark and trade secret (§6, §7) rather than assuming copyright carries the weight.

## 4. What Michelle can and can't use in her materials

**Standards text.** State standards are generally government works and freely usable. Common Core-derived text carries a public license requiring attribution — the standard notice is `© Copyright 2010. National Governors Association Center for Best Practices and Council of Chief State School Officers. All rights reserved.` Maryland CCRS descends from CCSS; include appropriate attribution. Quoting standard codes and text for alignment purposes is routine and expected.

**Released state test items and blueprints.** MCAP released items and blueprints are published for educator use, but "released for educator use" is not the same as "licensed for commercial republication." Check the usage terms on the specific documents before reproducing item text in a paid product. Using blueprints as *structural reference* — DOK distribution, item-type mix, standards weighting — is analysis of publicly available information and is a different matter from reproducing item text.

**Reading passages.** Original passages avoid the problem entirely, which is why generating them is the right call. If a published excerpt is ever wanted: **fair use is weak here.** The use is commercial, the works are typically creative, and reproducing a passage for a reading comprehension product substitutes for the licensed anthology market — that's the fourth factor, and it's the one courts weight most heavily. Educational purpose does not save a commercial reproduction. License it or write original.

**Images.** The design system already mandates public-domain imagery. Maintain the sourcing record: file, source institution, URL, date accessed, basis for public domain status. U.S. government works (National Archives, Marine Corps archives, Library of Congress) are the reliable source. Be careful with images labeled "public domain" on aggregator sites — the label is often wrong, and reliance on it isn't a defense.

**Fonts.** Fonts are licensed software; the license governs embedding, PDF distribution, and commercial use. The design system calls for embedding brand fonts in .pptx files — **confirm the license permits embedding and redistribution**. This is a genuinely common and genuinely overlooked violation in template products.

**Other teachers' materials.** Obvious, but: no adapting TPT purchases, district-provided curriculum, or colleagues' materials into product. The layout and organizational structure of a worksheet can be protectable even where individual facts aren't.

**Historical and factual content.** Facts aren't copyrightable. The *expression* of facts is. Research widely, write in her own words, and cite sources — which is also good practice pedagogically.

## 5. Licensing

### HallPass — the arrangement with Chris Carlozzi

The business skill records: Chris retains IP, Michelle holds an exclusive marketing license, 50/50 revenue split after hosting costs.

**⚠️ First question every time this comes up: is this in a signed written agreement?** An exclusive copyright license is required to be in writing and signed by the rights owner (17 U.S.C. §204(a)). An unwritten "exclusive license" is at best a non-exclusive implied license and at worst nothing. Without paper, Michelle has no enforceable exclusivity, no protection if Chris licenses to someone else, and no standing to sue infringers.

The agreement needs to address:
- **Scope** — exclusive as to what? Territory, market segment, field of use, duration. "Exclusive marketing license" is ambiguous; does it cover K-12 nationally, or DMV districts, or all sectors?
- **Sublicensing** — can she grant districts the license they need to actually use the software? If not, the arrangement doesn't function.
- **Revenue definition** — "50/50 after hosting costs" needs defined terms: which costs, whose accounting, what reporting cadence, audit rights.
- **Term and termination** — what triggers termination, what notice, and critically: **what happens to existing district contracts and customer relationships** if it ends. Michelle will have signed multi-year district agreements; she needs the right to continue servicing them.
- **Maintenance and support obligations** — who fixes it, on what SLA. She's the one signing district contracts; she needs to be able to meet them.
- **Source code escrow** — if Chris becomes unavailable, can she keep the districts running? For a product sold to institutions on annual contracts, this is not paranoia; districts sometimes require it.
- **What happens on death or incapacity** of either party.
- **Which entity is the counterparty** — The Rooted Learner LLC, not Michelle personally.
- **Data and privacy responsibilities** — who is the FERPA school official, who signs the DPA, who owns the breach obligation. Right now the district contract runs through Michelle, which means the compliance liability does too, while the code is Chris's. That gap needs closing.

**This is the highest-priority contract in the business and it should go to counsel.** A revenue-generating product with an unwritten IP arrangement is a foreseeable dispute.

### Licensing to districts

Default position: districts receive a **license, not ownership**. A perpetual, non-exclusive, internal-use license gives a district everything it needs. Full assignment gives away the reusable asset. See `contracts-and-dpa.md` §3.

Define the license scope explicitly:
- Which schools, how many teachers, how many students
- Internal use only — no redistribution to other districts or posting publicly
- Modification rights (districts often want to adapt; usually fine, with attribution and no redistribution)
- What happens at termination — perpetual for materials already delivered, terminating for SaaS access

### Consumer/teacher licensing (TPT, own site)

Publish clear terms with every product: single-classroom use, no sharing outside the purchasing teacher's classroom, no uploading to public sites or district-wide servers, no reselling or claiming as their own, multi-license discounts for teams. This is standard and enforceable, and it gives a basis for takedowns.

**Read the marketplace terms.** TPT and similar platforms take a license to display, market, and distribute uploaded content. Understand what's being granted before uploading anything that also anchors a district product line.

### Anyone who is ever paid to contribute

An independent contractor **owns what they create by default** unless there's a written agreement assigning it. "Work for hire" language alone is insufficient for most categories — the agreement needs an **express present assignment** of all rights, plus a waiver of moral rights and a fallback license. This applies to a designer, a writer, a developer, a VA — anyone. Get the assignment signed *before* the work starts.

## 6. Trademark

**"The Rooted Learner"** has brand equity per the business skill, which makes it worth protecting properly.

- **Common law rights** already exist from use in commerce, but they're limited to the geographic area and market of actual use — a real constraint for a business selling nationally online.
- **Federal registration** with the USPTO provides nationwide constructive use, the ® symbol, a presumption of validity, access to federal court, and a basis for platform takedowns (TPT, Amazon, and Instagram enforcement all run smoother with a registration number).
- **Classes to consider:** Class 41 (educational services, publications, training) is the core. Class 9 (downloadable software) and Class 42 (SaaS) matter for AssessAlign and HallPass. Each class carries its own fee.
- **Search first.** Run a clearance search on the USPTO database before filing — "rooted," "roots," and learning-related marks are a crowded space in education, and a likelihood-of-confusion refusal wastes the filing fee. A proper clearance search by a trademark attorney costs less than a rebrand.
- **Also consider** registering "AssessAlign" and "HallPass" separately if they're going to carry their own market identity — though note "HallPass" is a fairly descriptive term in the school context, which makes it a weaker mark, and there may be existing users of the name.
- **Use it correctly** to avoid genericide: as an adjective with a generic noun ("The Rooted Learner® print packs"), consistently, with ™ before registration and ® after.
- **Domain and handles** — hold therootedlearner.com and the matching social handles; secure obvious variants defensively where cheap.

## 7. Trade secret and protecting the tech build

For software and methodology, **trade secret is often the stronger protection than copyright** — and it's free, but only if the secrecy is actually maintained.

**What qualifies as a trade secret here:**
- AssessAlign's item-generation methodology, prompt architecture, and standards-alignment logic
- The Lexile/BOY-MOY-EOY calibration framework
- The ELD overlay generation methodology
- District pricing structures, client lists, and pipeline data
- Anything in the skills library that encodes hard-won methodology

**What's required to keep it.** Trade secret protection under the Defend Trade Secrets Act (18 U.S.C. §1836) and Maryland's Uniform Trade Secrets Act requires the information to derive value from not being generally known **and** to be the subject of **reasonable measures to keep it secret**. If she doesn't take the measures, there's no trade secret to enforce.

Reasonable measures for a business this size:
- NDAs before any substantive discussion with collaborators, contractors, or prospective partners
- Confidentiality clauses in every district contract covering methodology, not just data
- Access controls on repositories and documents; don't put the methodology in a public repo
- Mark genuinely confidential documents as confidential — labeling is evidence
- Don't disclose the mechanism in marketing. Show what it does, not exactly how it works
- Off-board contractors properly: retrieve materials, revoke access, confirm in writing

**Software specifics:**
- **Copyright** covers the code as written. Register the code for the products that matter — the Copyright Office permits depositing redacted portions of source code specifically so registration doesn't destroy trade secrecy.
- **Open source licenses in the dependency tree.** Copyleft licenses (GPL, AGPL) can carry obligations that conflict with commercial distribution — AGPL in particular reaches network-accessed software, which is exactly what a SaaS is. Audit dependencies before a district deployment.
- **Patents** are almost certainly not worth it at this stage — expensive, slow, and software patents post-*Alice* are hard to obtain and hold. Revisit only if there's genuinely novel technical machinery and outside capital.
- **Own the infrastructure accounts.** Domains, hosting, repos, and app store accounts in the LLC's name with the LLC's email, not a personal account and not a collaborator's. Recovering a domain from a former collaborator is a bad month.

## 8. Enforcement — what to do when someone copies

This will happen. Teacher materials get shared. Escalate proportionately:

1. **Document first.** Screenshots with visible URLs and dates, archived copies, a written comparison of what matches. Do this before making contact — infringers delete.
2. **Assess what was actually taken.** Ideas and standards alignment aren't protectable. Expression, layout, and specific text are. Be honest about which it is; a demand letter over unprotectable material damages credibility.
3. **Platform takedown.** For TPT, Etsy, Instagram, or a hosted site, a **DMCA notice** (17 U.S.C. §512) to the platform's designated agent is usually faster and cheaper than anything else. Notices require specific elements including a good-faith statement and a statement under penalty of perjury — get them right. A registration number strengthens the notice considerably.
4. **Direct contact**, if it looks like a teacher who didn't understand licensing rather than a commercial pirate. A polite note citing the license terms resolves most of these, and preserves a relationship in a small professional community.
5. **Demand letter** from counsel for commercial infringement.
6. **Litigation** only where damages justify it — which, again, largely means where the work was **timely registered** and statutory damages are available. This is the practical reason §1 matters.

**Careful on the other side:** do not send aggressive demand letters without confirming ownership and registration status. A misfired claim against another educator travels fast in this market and the reputational cost exceeds the value of most single products. And note the reciprocal risk — the same care applies to making sure her own materials don't inadvertently copy someone else's.

### Take to counsel
- Trademark clearance search and filing strategy for "The Rooted Learner," and whether to file for AssessAlign/HallPass.
- The HallPass licensing agreement with Chris Carlozzi — **highest priority**.
- A written assignment of pre-LLC works from Michelle to The Rooted Learner LLC.
- Whether the AI-assisted authorship position supports registration, and how to characterize human contribution on the applications.
- A contractor IP assignment template to have ready before hiring anyone.
